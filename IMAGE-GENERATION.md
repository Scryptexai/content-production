IMAGE GENERATION & ASSET DIRECTION

1. Core Philosophy

Image generation is not about producing beautiful pictures.

It is about producing usable visual assets that serve a specific storytelling purpose.

An image can look impressive but still be unsuitable for video production because:

- The composition does not support the intended shot.
- The subject is positioned incorrectly.
- Important objects are cropped.
- The background contains distracting details.
- The visual style changes between scenes.
- The image cannot be separated into useful animation layers.
- The generated image contradicts the actual story.

A production-ready image must be designed for its intended use, not merely for visual appeal.

---

2. Understand the Asset Before Generating It

Before generating an image, identify its production role.

Asset Categories

Category| Purpose
Character Asset| People, characters, expressions, poses
Environment Asset| Rooms, streets, buildings, landscapes
Object Asset| Phones, documents, vehicles, furniture
Background Asset| Atmospheric or contextual scene backgrounds
Visual Metaphor| Visual representation of abstract ideas
Evidence Asset| Verified reference material, documents, charts
Motion Graphics Asset| Shapes, diagrams, symbols, interface elements
Transition Asset| Visual elements used to connect scenes

Different asset categories require different generation strategies.

For example:

A character intended for animation needs a clear silhouette, readable pose, and sufficient separation from surrounding elements.

A cinematic background needs environmental depth, lighting, and composition that leave room for the main subject.

A visual metaphor needs immediate conceptual clarity.

---

3. Art Direction Before Image Generation

Every video needs a coherent visual identity.

The agent must establish an art direction before generating production assets.

Art Direction Specification

Define:

1. Visual style.
2. Illustration technique.
3. Color palette.
4. Lighting direction.
5. Contrast and saturation.
6. Character design language.
7. Environment design language.
8. Texture and detail level.
9. Composition principles.
10. Aspect ratio and framing.

Example Art Direction

Project style:

- 2D illustrated storytelling.
- Modern editorial illustration.
- Hand-drawn sketch elements combined with polished motion graphics.
- Strong silhouettes.
- Controlled visual textures.
- Cinematic composition.
- Expressive character poses.
- Clear foreground, middle ground, and background.
- Consistent color language.

This is an example direction, not a mandatory style for every project.

The agent must select a style that supports the subject and audience.

Important Rule

Do not change art direction randomly between shots.

A scene may use different compositions, environments, or colors while maintaining the same visual identity.

---

4. Prompt Engineering for Production Assets

Image prompts must describe the intended production result.

Avoid prompts that only contain generic quality keywords such as:

- Masterpiece.
- Ultra detailed.
- Beautiful.
- Cinematic.
- Professional.
- 4K.

These words do not adequately define the required composition or storytelling function.

Production Prompt Structure

Use this framework:

A. Subject
What must appear?

B. Narrative Context
What is happening and why?

C. Action / Pose
What is the subject doing?

D. Environment
Where does the scene take place?

E. Composition
Where should the subject and important elements appear?

F. Camera
What perspective and framing are required?

G. Lighting
What lighting supports the scene?

H. Art Direction
What visual style must be maintained?

I. Color
What palette and contrast are required?

J. Production Constraints
What must be avoided to keep the asset usable?

Example

Narrative purpose:

Show emotional distance between two people.

Production prompt:

"Create a 2D editorial illustration showing two human characters sitting at opposite ends of a long sofa inside a modern apartment. Both characters face away from each other. The composition emphasizes the empty space between them. Wide cinematic framing, clear character silhouettes, layered foreground and background, expressive body language, restrained lighting, consistent modern editorial illustration style. Leave sufficient negative space for future camera movement. Do not include captions, subtitles, logos, watermarks, or decorative typography."

The prompt describes a usable scene, not merely a subject.

5. Composition and Negative Space

Composition determines how an image communicates.

The agent must consider:

- Subject placement.
- Visual hierarchy.
- Foreground and background.
- Negative space.
- Direction of gaze.
- Direction of movement.
- Balance.
- Depth.
- Screen-safe areas.

Composition Techniques

Rule of Thirds

Place important subjects away from the exact center when appropriate.

Leading Lines

Use environmental lines to guide the audience's attention.

Symmetry

Useful for institutional environments, confrontation, or visual comparison.

Negative Space

Create breathing room around subjects and important objects.

Foreground Framing

Use foreground elements to create depth and direct attention.

Visual Contrast

Use differences in scale, color, lighting, or position to communicate relationships.

Composition must support the intended shot.

Do not apply the same layout to every generated image.

6. Layer Separation and Animation Readiness

A single flattened image can limit animation possibilities.

When a shot requires movement, the agent should consider whether the asset needs separate layers.

Potential layers:

- Background.
- Midground.
- Foreground.
- Main character.
- Secondary character.
- Important objects.
- Shadows.
- Lighting effects.
- Motion graphics.

Example

Scene:

A person sitting in a room looking at a phone.

Possible layers:

1. Room background.
2. Window and exterior environment.
3. Furniture.
4. Main character.
5. Character's arm and phone, if separate movement is required.
6. Foreground objects.
7. Lighting effects.

These layers allow:

- Parallax.
- Camera movement.
- Independent object animation.
- Lighting changes.
- Focus transitions.

Important Limitation

Do not assume an image generator can produce clean, independently editable layers.

If the tool does not support layer separation, use an appropriate image-editing or compositing workflow.

Verify the actual output before planning animation around it.

7. Character Consistency

Recurring characters must maintain a recognizable identity.

Define a character reference specification:

- Character ID.
- Age range, where editorially appropriate.
- General appearance.
- Hairstyle.
- Clothing.
- Body proportions.
- Distinguishing features.
- Color palette.
- Illustration style.
- Expression range.
- Pose requirements.

Use reference images or supported image-conditioning features when available.

Character Consistency Rules

- Do not redesign a recurring character without a narrative reason.
- Maintain clothing continuity within the same timeline.
- Keep distinguishing features consistent.
- Match proportions and illustration style.
- Check facial and body consistency between generated assets.

For fictional characters, visual continuity is a creative requirement.

For real public figures, use accurate and clearly contextualized representations. Do not fabricate visual evidence or depict invented events as authentic footage.

---

8. Reference Images and Research Integrity

Reference imagery serves different purposes.

Reference Types

Style Reference
Used to maintain visual language.

Character Reference
Used to maintain fictional character consistency.

Composition Reference
Used to guide framing and subject placement.

Historical / Documentary Reference
Used to understand real events, places, or objects.

Evidence Reference
Used when a visual claim depends on an actual source.

Never confuse these categories.

A generated image is not evidence that an event happened.

When discussing current news or politics:

- Verify important visual claims.
- Preserve original context.
- Do not fabricate documents, headlines, photographs, or quotations.
- Clearly distinguish illustrative scenes from authentic reference material.
- Do not use fictional imagery in a way that misleads the audience about real events.

---

9. Asset Quality Control

Every generated asset must pass a review before entering production.

Technical Review

Check:

- Resolution.
- Aspect ratio.
- File format.
- Cropping.
- Visual artifacts.
- Character anatomy.
- Object consistency.
- Background quality.
- Transparency, if required.
- Compatibility with the intended editing workflow.

Creative Review

Check:

- Does the image serve its intended shot?
- Is the main subject immediately recognizable?
- Is the composition effective?
- Does it match the art direction?
- Can it be animated if necessary?
- Does it add meaningful visual information?
- Does it conflict with another scene?

An image should not be accepted simply because it looks attractive.

Asset Status

Classify each asset:

- APPROVED: Ready for production.
- REVISION REQUIRED: Useful but needs correction.
- REJECTED: Does not meet production requirements.
- REFERENCE ONLY: Useful for guidance, not final production.

10. Asset Naming and Organization

Use a predictable naming system.

Example:

PROJECT/

- 00_ART_DIRECTION/
- 01_CHARACTER_REFERENCES/
- 02_ENVIRONMENTS/
- 03_OBJECTS/
- 04_SCENE_ASSETS/
- 05_MOTION_GRAPHICS/
- 06_AUDIO/
- 07_STORYBOARD/
- 08_FINAL_RENDER/

Example filenames:

- CHAR_001_MAIN_CHARACTER.png
- ENV_001_APARTMENT_WIDE.png
- OBJ_001_SMARTPHONE.png
- SCN_003_EMOTIONAL_DISTANCE.png
- MG_002_INFORMATION_FLOW.png

Keep source assets separate from edited and final assets.

Do not overwrite approved source files during revisions.

11. Practical Exercise

Topic:

"Kenapa orang bisa merasa kesepian meskipun selalu terhubung dengan banyak orang?"

Create an asset package for a short visual storytelling sequence.

Requirements:

1. One consistent fictional main character.
2. Two different environments.
3. Three distinct visual compositions.
4. One visual metaphor.
5. At least one image designed for parallax or camera movement.
6. At least one asset prepared for separate-layer animation.
7. One consistent art direction specification.
8. Asset naming and quality-control records.

Do not generate all assets randomly.

Start with the art direction, character reference, and storyboard requirements.

Then generate assets according to their actual production roles.

Score each criterion from 0 to 5.

Criterion| Evaluation
Art Direction| Is the visual identity coherent?

Composition| Are subjects and visual hierarchy well designed?
Character Consistency| Are recurring characters recognizable?

Asset Quality| Are images technically and creatively usable?
Organization| Are assets properly named and managed?

Passing score: 26/35.

Critical failure:

If the agent generates attractive but unrelated images that cannot support the storyboard, the production package cannot pass.

---

13. Definition of Done

The agent has mastered this knowledge when it can:

- Establish a coherent art direction.
- Identify the correct asset category.
- Write production-oriented image prompts.
- Design compositions for specific shots.
- Maintain character consistency.
- Prepare images for animation.
- Distinguish illustrative imagery from documentary evidence.
- Review assets before production.
- Organize assets systematically.
- Explain why each asset exists in the video.

Final Principle:

Do not generate images to fill the video.

Generate assets to make the story visually possible.

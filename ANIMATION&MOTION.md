ANIMATION & MOTION GRAPHICS DIRECTION

1. Core Philosophy

Animation is not decoration.

Animation is a storytelling mechanism that makes visual information move, change, interact, and develop.

A static image shows a moment.

Animation can show:

- What happened before that moment.
- What is happening now.
- What changes afterward.
- How two elements interact.
- How an abstract idea develops.
- How the audience should interpret a visual event.

The goal is not to make everything move. The goal is to make meaningful things happen.

---

2. Animation Thinking

Before animating an asset, define:

1. What is the starting state?
2. What changes?
3. What causes the change?
4. What should the audience notice?
5. What is the ending state?
6. Does the movement communicate meaning?

Example: Information Spreading

Static concept:
A smartphone displaying a social media post.

Animation sequence:

1. A new post appears on the phone.
2. The phone vibrates.
3. The post duplicates into several screens.
4. Screens spread across the composition.
5. Reactions begin appearing.
6. The composition becomes crowded.

The animation communicates information propagation.

It does not need a paragraph of text explaining what is happening.

3. Motion Design Fundamentals

The agent should understand the following motion principles.

A. Timing

Timing determines how long an action takes.

- Fast movement: urgency, impact, surprise.
- Slow movement: reflection, tension, emotional weight.
- Gradual movement: transformation or discovery.
- Sudden movement: interruption or emphasis.

Do not use identical timing for every animation.

B. Spacing

Spacing describes how movement progresses between frames.

Even movement can feel mechanical.

Controlled acceleration and deceleration often create more natural motion.

C. Easing

Common motion curves:

- Ease In: movement starts slowly and accelerates.
- Ease Out: movement decelerates toward the end.
- Ease In-Out: gradual acceleration and deceleration.
- Linear: constant speed.
- Overshoot: movement briefly exceeds its destination.
- Bounce: movement reacts with a bouncing effect.

Choose easing according to the subject and intended emotion.

D. Anticipation

A small preparatory movement before a major action.

Examples:

- A character leans backward before moving forward.
- A graphic element contracts before expanding.
- A camera pauses before revealing a large environment.

E. Follow-Through

Movement does not always stop simultaneously.

Examples:

- Hair continues moving after a character stops.
- A document continues sliding after being released.
- Small particles settle after an impact.

F. Secondary Motion

Supporting movement that makes the main action feel more believable.

Examples:

- Moving shadows.
- Floating particles.
- Clothing movement.
- Environmental movement.
- Small interface reactions.

Secondary motion must not distract from the primary action.

4. Camera Animation

Camera movement is one of the most important tools for illustrated storytelling.

Push In

Move closer to a subject.

Useful for:

- Emotional emphasis.
- Important details.
- Realization.
- Increasing tension.

Pull Out

Move away from a subject.

Useful for:

- Revealing context.
- Showing isolation.
- Revealing scale.
- Changing perspective.

Pan

Move horizontally through a composition.

Useful for:

- Following an action.
- Revealing a larger environment.
- Connecting visual elements.

Tilt

Move vertically through a composition.

Useful for:

- Revealing height.
- Showing scale.
- Connecting different levels of an environment.

Parallax

Move different visual layers at different speeds to simulate depth.

Useful for:

- Illustrated environments.
- City scenes.
- Character compositions.
- Atmospheric backgrounds.

Camera Rules

- Camera movement must support the narrative.
- Do not zoom continuously without purpose.
- Do not apply identical camera movement to every shot.
- Avoid movement that crops important subjects.
- Preserve readable composition throughout the movement.

5. 2D Parallax and Layer Animation

Parallax is particularly useful for 2D illustrated videos.

A scene can be divided into:

1. Background.
2. Distant environment.
3. Middle ground.
4. Main subject.
5. Foreground.
6. Atmospheric effects.

Each layer moves at a different rate.

Example

A city illustration:

- Background buildings move slowly.
- Middle-ground buildings move slightly faster.
- Foreground objects move faster.
- Main character remains the focal point.
- Atmospheric particles move independently.

This creates depth from a flat illustration.

Important Constraints

Parallax requires sufficient image coverage.

If the camera reveals areas outside the original image, the production may expose:

- Empty regions.
- Missing objects.
- Repeated textures.
- Distorted edges.

The agent must inspect the composition and prepare additional background areas when necessary.

Do not assume that every image is suitable for parallax.

6. Character Animation

Characters should communicate through body language, not just facial expressions.

Possible animation elements:

- Head movement.
- Eye direction.
- Hand gestures.
- Body posture.
- Walking.
- Turning.
- Sitting.
- Standing.
- Object interaction.
- Facial expression changes.

Example: Emotional Disconnection

Sequence:

1. Character A attempts to communicate.
2. Character B looks away.
3. Character A pauses.
4. Character B checks their phone.
5. Character A slowly changes posture.
6. The distance between them becomes the visual focus.

The movement communicates the emotional situation.

Important Rule

Do not animate every body part continuously.

Small, controlled movements can communicate more than exaggerated gestures.

If the available tools cannot produce convincing character animation, use alternative techniques such as:

- Pose changes.
- Cutout animation.
- Layer transformation.
- Camera movement.
- Carefully composed sequential illustrations.

Never claim full character animation when only camera movement was applied.

---

7. Motion Graphics

Motion graphics should transform information into visual experiences.

A. Kinetic Typography

Use typography when words themselves are important.

Examples:

- A critical quotation.
- An important statement.
- A key term.
- A number.
- A short phrase that deserves emphasis.

Avoid displaying entire narration paragraphs.

B. Animated Diagrams

Useful for explaining:

- Social structures.
- Cause and effect.
- Relationships.
- Processes.
- Information flows.
- Decision-making systems.

C. Data Visualization

Use charts and graphs when real data supports the argument.

Requirements:

- Verify source data.
- Preserve correct values.
- Use appropriate scales.
- Label important information.
- Avoid misleading visual proportions.

D. Interface Animation

Useful for demonstrating:

- Social media interactions.
- Information spreading.
- Notifications.
- Digital communication.
- Online reactions.

Do not fabricate authentic-looking screenshots or interfaces that could be mistaken for evidence.

E. Shape Transformation

Use shape transformations to connect concepts.

Example:

A single speech bubble transforms into multiple overlapping speech bubbles, representing the spread of opinions.

The transformation should communicate a conceptual relationship.

---

8. Transitions and Motion Continuity

Transitions must connect visual events.

Useful techniques:

Match Movement
An object moving in one shot continues its movement in the next.

Shape Transition
One shape transforms into another.

Camera Transition
Camera movement reveals the next scene.

Mask Transition
An object or shape reveals the next composition.

Graphic Transition
A graphic element becomes part of the next visual environment.

Cut on Action
Cut between shots while an action is occurring.

Do not add elaborate transitions between every shot.

Sometimes a direct cut is more effective.

---

9. Motion Rhythm

Animation must follow the emotional rhythm of the story.

Narrative Moment| Motion Direction
Opening Hook| Immediate, controlled visual movement
Context| Stable, readable movement
Conflict| Increasing motion or visual contrast
Revelation| Reveal, transformation, or sudden shift
Emotional Peak| Focused, deliberate movement
Reflection| Reduced movement and visual breathing room
Ending| A meaningful final visual state

These are guidelines, not mandatory formulas.

A quiet static shot may be more effective than a dramatic animation.

---

10. Sound and Motion Synchronization

Sound effects should support visible actions.

Examples:

- Notification sound when a message appears.
- Impact sound when a visual element collides.
- Subtle whoosh during a meaningful transition.
- Ambient sound to establish an environment.
- Silence before an important reveal.

Avoid:

- Excessive whoosh effects.
- Unnecessary impacts.
- Random sound effects.
- Constant background noise.
- Music that overwhelms narration.

Audio and animation should work together.

---

11. Production Workflow

Follow this sequence:

Stage 1 — Motion Planning

For every shot, identify:

- Starting visual state.
- Main action.
- Supporting movement.
- Camera movement.
- Ending visual state.

Stage 2 — Technical Preparation

Inspect:

- Available image layers.
- Image resolution.
- Asset dimensions.
- Animation capabilities.
- Timeline support.
- Rendering limitations.

Stage 3 — Animate

Apply movement according to the storyboard.

Do not invent unnecessary motion.

Stage 4 — Review

Check:

- Movement quality.
- Timing.
- Easing.
- Visual continuity.
- Cropping.
- Motion consistency.
- Narration synchronization.

Stage 5 — Render Test

Render a short sample before rendering the entire video.

Verify that the actual output matches the intended motion.

12. Practical Exercise

Topic:

"Bagaimana satu komentar bisa memicu konflik yang semakin besar di media sosial?"

Produce a 30–45 second animated sequence.

Requirements:

- At least 5 distinct visual beats.
- At least 2 different camera movements.
- At least 1 parallax composition.
- At least 1 animated information flow.
- At least 1 meaningful visual transition.
- At least 1 moment using reduced motion or a static composition.
- Sound effects synchronized with important visual actions.
- No continuous full-sentence kinetic typography.

The agent must provide:

1. Motion storyboard.
2. Asset requirements.
3. Animation plan.
4. Tool usage record.
5. Rendered test.
6. Final review.

13. Evaluation

Score each criterion from 0 to 5.

Criterion| Evaluation
Motion Purpose| Does movement communicate meaning?
Timing & Easing| Does movement feel intentional?
Camera Direction| Does the camera support storytelling?
Layer Animation| Are layers used effectively?
Character / Object Motion| Are actions understandable?
Motion Graphics| Does animation explain or emphasize information?
Audio Synchronization| Do sound and movement work together?

Passing score: 26/35.

Critical failure:

If the animation consists primarily of repeated zooms, pans, fades, and decorative effects without meaningful visual development, it cannot pass.

---

14. Definition of Done

The agent has mastered this knowledge when it can:

- Design motion before animating.
- Use timing and easing appropriately.
- Direct camera movement.
- Produce layered 2D motion.
- Apply parallax correctly.
- Animate characters and objects within actual tool capabilities.
- Create informative motion graphics.
- Design meaningful transitions.
- Synchronize movement with sound.
- Render and evaluate an actual animation sample.

Final Principle:

Movement is not the objective.

Meaningful visual change is the objective.

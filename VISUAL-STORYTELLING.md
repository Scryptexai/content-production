VISUAL STORYTELLING & SHOT DESIGN

1. Core Principle

A video is not a collection of images placed behind narration.

A video is a sequence of visual events designed to communicate meaning.

Every shot must contribute at least one of these:

- Introduce information.
- Establish a situation.
- Show an action.
- Reveal a contradiction.
- Explain a relationship.
- Communicate an emotion.
- Demonstrate a consequence.
- Shift the audience's perspective.

If a shot does none of these, reconsider its existence.

The objective is not to illustrate every sentence. The objective is to make the audience understand and experience the story through visuals.

---

2. Think in Visual Events, Not Sentences

The agent must distinguish between:

- SCRIPT: What is being said.
- VISUAL BEAT: What the audience needs to see.
- SHOT: How that visual beat is presented.
- MOTION: What changes during the shot.

One sentence may require multiple shots.

Multiple sentences may be communicated through one continuous shot.

Example

SCRIPT:

"Orang sering merasa sudah memahami sebuah masalah hanya karena mereka melihat satu potongan video."

Weak approach:

- Display the entire sentence.
- Show a generic person holding a phone.
- Animate the sentence word by word.

Better visual approach:

Beat 1 — Limited Information

- Close-up of a phone.
- A video is playing.
- The frame shows only a small portion of a larger situation.

Beat 2 — Premature Conclusion

- A character watches the video.
- Their expression changes.
- A conclusion appears as a visual reaction, not a written sentence.

Beat 3 — Missing Context

- Camera pulls back.
- The environment reveals that the original video showed only part of the event.

Beat 4 — Perspective Shift

- The complete situation becomes visible.
- The audience realizes that the initial interpretation was incomplete.

The visual sequence demonstrates the argument rather than simply repeating it.

3. Shot Composition

The agent must understand the purpose of different shot sizes.

Shot Type| Primary Function
Extreme Wide Shot| Establish scale, environment, isolation
Wide Shot| Show the complete situation and relationships
Medium Shot| Show interaction and body language
Medium Close-Up| Show reactions and emotional context
Close-Up| Emphasize important details
Extreme Close-Up| Reveal small but meaningful details
Over-the-Shoulder| Show perspective or interaction
Top-Down Shot| Explain spatial relationships or systems
POV Shot| Place the audience inside an experience

Do not select camera angles randomly.

A shot size must support the information or emotion being communicated.

Example: Relationship Conflict

- Wide shot: Two people sitting far apart.
- Medium shot: One person tries to speak.
- Close-up: The other person avoids eye contact.
- Extreme close-up: A phone receives a message.
- Wide shot: The distance between them becomes the dominant visual element.

The camera helps communicate emotional progression.

---

4. Camera Movement

Camera movement should have narrative motivation.

Movement| Possible Purpose
Push In| Increase attention or emotional intensity
Pull Out| Reveal context or create distance
Pan| Discover information or follow action
Tilt| Reveal scale or vertical relationships
Tracking| Follow a character or object
Orbit| Reveal a subject from multiple perspectives
Static Shot| Allow observation, tension, or reflection
Parallax| Create depth in illustrated compositions
Rack Focus| Shift attention between subjects

These are creative possibilities, not fixed rules.

Important Rule

Do not animate every shot.

A static composition can be more powerful than constant camera movement.

Movement without purpose creates visual noise.

---

5. Visual Metaphor

Visual metaphor allows abstract ideas to become visible.

This is particularly important for social commentary, relationships, politics, and social phenomena.

Abstract Idea| Possible Visual Metaphor
Loss of trust| A cracked transparent surface between two people
Social pressure| A person surrounded by identical pointing figures
Information manipulation| A distorted reflection of an original image
Economic inequality| Two contrasting environments separated by a visible barrier
Political accountability| A chain of decisions leading to ordinary people
Emotional distance| Two characters separated by an expanding empty space
Viral misinformation| One small distorted object multiplying across screens
Confirmation bias| A character looking through a filter that only reveals selected information

A metaphor must clarify the idea.

Avoid using generic symbols simply because they look cinematic.

Metaphor Validation

Ask:

1. What does the visual represent?
2. Is the relationship understandable?
3. Does it support the actual argument?
4. Could it accidentally imply something factually inaccurate?
5. Is there a more direct visual explanation?

Do not use metaphor to replace evidence when discussing real events or political claims.

6. Visual Progression and Continuity

A sequence should feel connected.

The agent must consider:

- Subject continuity.
- Character consistency.
- Environment continuity.
- Color and lighting consistency.
- Object positions.
- Direction of movement.
- Camera perspective.
- Transition logic.

A scene transition should connect ideas, actions, environments, or emotional states.

Useful Transition Methods

Match Cut
Connect two visually similar shapes, objects, or movements.

Graphic Match
Use a shared composition or visual form to connect scenes.

Movement Transition
Use movement from one shot to lead into another.

Reveal Transition
Gradually reveal information that changes the audience's understanding.

Environment Transition
Move between locations through a meaningful visual connection.

Contrast Cut
Move directly between opposing situations to emphasize differences.

Avoid using the same transition repeatedly without a creative reason.

7. Storyboard Specification

Before production, every important shot must have a storyboard description.

Use this structure:

Field| Description
Shot ID| Unique identifier
Narrative Beat| What part of the story this shot represents
Purpose| Why the shot exists
Visual Description| What the audience sees
Composition| Subject placement and framing
Camera| Shot size, angle, movement
Action| What changes during the shot
Motion Graphics| Additional animated elements
Text| Essential on-screen text only
Audio| Narration, music, sound effects
Duration| Estimated shot length
Assets| Required visual resources
Production Method| Which verified tools will create it

The storyboard must be specific enough for another production agent to execute without inventing the central creative direction.

---

8. Visual Diversity

Do not let every scene use the same composition.

A video can become monotonous even when its images are technically different.

Visual diversity can come from:

- Different shot sizes.
- Different camera angles.
- Different environments.
- Character interactions.
- Object-focused scenes.
- Abstract visual metaphors.
- Animated diagrams.
- Contrasting compositions.
- Different motion patterns.
- Changes in visual density.

However, diversity must not destroy visual identity.

Maintain consistent art direction across the entire video.

---

9. Text and Motion Graphics Integration

Motion graphics should explain, emphasize, or transform information.

Good uses:

- A timeline showing how an event developed.
- A diagram revealing a relationship.
- A number appearing at the moment it becomes relevant.
- A map identifying a location.
- A quotation presented as evidence.
- A comparison showing two different outcomes.

Weak uses:

- Every spoken sentence animated as typography.
- Random floating words.
- Repeated kinetic text without informational purpose.
- Decorative graphs with no meaningful data.
- Large captions occupying the entire screen unnecessarily.

Rule

Motion graphics must add visual information, not merely animate existing text.

---

10. Practical Exercise

Topic:

"Kenapa sebuah konflik kecil bisa berubah menjadi masalah besar di media sosial?"

Create a 30–45 second storyboard.

Requirements:

- Minimum 6 shots.
- Minimum 4 different shot compositions.
- At least 1 visual metaphor.
- At least 1 meaningful camera movement.
- At least 1 visual reveal.
- At least 1 motion graphics sequence.
- No full-sentence narration displayed as text.
- Every shot must have a clear narrative purpose.

The agent must deliver the storyboard before generating assets.

---

11. Evaluation

Score each criterion from 0 to 5.

Criterion| Evaluation
Visual Interpretation| Does the visual communicate the intended idea?
Composition| Is the framing deliberate and effective?
Camera Direction| Does camera movement have a purpose?
Visual Metaphor| Are abstract ideas represented clearly?
Continuity| Do shots connect naturally?
Visual Diversity| Does the sequence avoid monotonous compositions?
Production Readiness| Can another agent execute the storyboard?

Passing score: 26/35.

Critical failure:

If the storyboard is merely a list of images matching individual narration sentences, it cannot pass.

---

12. Definition of Done

The agent has mastered this knowledge when it can:

- Convert a script into visual beats.
- Design shots independently from sentence boundaries.
- Choose appropriate shot sizes and camera angles.
- Direct meaningful camera movement.
- Create understandable visual metaphors.
- Maintain visual continuity.
- Design varied compositions.
- Integrate motion graphics without excessive typography.
- Produce an executable storyboard before asset generation.

Final Principle:

Do not ask what image should accompany the sentence.

Ask what the audience should see to understand the idea.
